\begin{abstract}
Compact EEG decoders summarise each window as log power per channel, which is the diagonal of its spatial covariance. How channels covary is discarded, and on movement-intent tasks that structure carries information the diagonal does not.

We add a tangent-space branch beside the log-power stem. Each window's covariance is shrunk, mapped to the tangent space at a reference the network maintains from the input alone, and projected into the classifier. No labels are used at inference and the reference keeps updating on the test stream. Across five cohorts and 51 participants, three seeds each, the branch raises balanced accuracy on four: $+0.064$ on a 20-participant motor-execution cohort held out from all development ($p=8.8\times10^{-5}$, higher in 20 of 20), $+0.021$ on a lower-limb exoskeleton cohort ($p=0.047$), $+0.040$ on BCI Competition IV-2a ($p=0.020$), and $+0.045$ on a second exoskeleton cohort. It beats all eight published decoders trained in the same pipeline on each cohort where the comparison was run.

On the fifth cohort it costs $0.175$ and falls near chance, and that failure is predictable before training. The branch's reference is a running mean over the incoming windows, so it is safe only while a single covariance update spans more than one class block. A block lasts 214 windows there against an update of 32; on the four cohorts where the branch helps a block is 1 to 18 windows and every update is class-mixed by construction. Both quantities are read from the recording protocol, neither is fitted, and the comparison separates all five cohorts. Enlarging the update to span a block recovers 74\,% of the loss, which is causal support for the account without restoring parity. A stronger prescription, setting the adaptation rate from the block length, was pre-registered and falsified.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Spinal cord injury leaves roughly 250{,}000 to 500{,}000 people per year with
impaired or absent lower-limb function, and powered exoskeletons driven by
non-invasive brain signals are one route to restoring overground mobility
\citep{he_exo,lopezlarraz,do}. Decoding the wearer's intent to walk or to stop
from scalp EEG is the control problem at the centre of such systems
\citep{contrerasvidal,presacco}. It is not well described by per-epoch
classification accuracy alone, for three reasons that shape the design presented
here.

\paragraph{Longitudinal drift} The decoder is fitted once and must keep working
weeks later. In the primary cohort used here this is literal: the model is
fitted on sessions 1--3 and evaluated on sessions 4--9, recorded across weeks.
Electrode montage, impedance and skin contact all change between sessions, and
the resulting shift in second-order statistics is the dominant source of
non-stationarity in EEG decoding \citep{shenoy,krauledat,blankertz2008}. Session
drift degrades fixed spatial filters and is the reason most BCI systems require
a per-session calibration block \citep{vidaurre}.

\paragraph{Asymmetric cost} A false Walk command moves a person's legs. Balanced
accuracy scores that identically to a missed Walk and cannot distinguish one
sustained false activation from fifty scattered false windows. The quantity a
wearer experiences is spurious activations per minute of standing, which we
report alongside accuracy throughout.

\paragraph{What a log-power stem cannot see} Compact EEG decoders reduce each window to log power per channel after spatial filtering \citep{schirrmeister,eegnet}. That summary is the diagonal of the window's spatial covariance. The off-diagonal entries, how channels covary, are discarded, and covariance structure is the basis of the Riemannian methods that remain competitive on movement-intent tasks \citep{barachant,congedo}. Whether a convolutional decoder is losing usable information at that step is an empirical question, and it is the one this paper answers.

Existing responses to drift are mostly applied offline in preprocessing.
Euclidean Alignment whitens each recording by the inverse square root of its
mean covariance \citep{hewu}; Riemannian Procrustes analysis and parallel
transport generalise this to the manifold of symmetric positive-definite (SPD)
matrices \citep{rodrigues,yair,zanini}. All of these estimate the alignment
statistic over a complete recording, which means the target recording must exist
before the decoder can run on it. Test-time adaptation removes that requirement
in computer vision by updating normalisation statistics or parameters online
\citep{tent,schneider,nado}, and recent work shows that such methods degrade
when the test stream is temporally correlated instead of i.i.d.
\citep{note,sar,cotta}. EEG data acquired under a blocked experimental protocol
is temporally correlated by construction, which is the connection this paper
makes precise.

Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Add a tangent-space branch beside the log-power stem: each window's covariance is shrunk, mapped to the tangent space at a reference estimated from the input alone, and projected into the classifier, with no labels used at inference (Section~\ref{sec:tangent}).
\item Measure it on five cohorts and 51 participants with three seeds each. Accuracy rises on four, including $+0.064$ in 20 of 20 participants of a cohort held out from every design decision (Section~\ref{sec:fivecohort}).
\item Compare against eight published decoders trained in one pipeline with shared splits, seeds and metric code, and report the cohort where most of them fail to train rather than letting the resulting margins stand (Section~\ref{sec:newbaselines}).
\item Identify the branch's failure mode and predict it from the protocol. The reference is a running mean over incoming windows, so it is safe only while one covariance update spans more than one class block; on the cohort where a block outlasts an update the branch costs $0.175$ and falls near chance (Condition~\ref{prop:band}, Section~\ref{sec:rate}).
\item Test that account causally by enlarging the covariance update at fixed block length, which recovers 74\,% of the loss without restoring parity (Section~\ref{sec:fivecohort}).
\item Pre-register a stronger version of the same condition, one that sets the adaptation rate from the block length, and report it as falsified (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}).
\end{enumerate}

The branch costs parameters. It adds roughly 271{,}000 to a 20{,}000-parameter stem, so the reported model is no longer compact and we do not claim parameter efficiency; Section~\ref{sec:compare} reports the counts beside the accuracies so the trade is visible.

\section{Related work}

\subsection{Compact convolutional decoders for EEG}

ShallowFBCSPNet and Deep4Net established that a
$\mathrm{square} \rightarrow \mathrm{pool} \rightarrow \log$ pipeline over
learned spatial filters recovers the band-power features that filter-bank common
spatial patterns computes explicitly \citep{schirrmeister}. EEGNet reduced the
parameter count by an order of magnitude using depthwise-separable convolutions
\citep{lawhern}, and EEGNeX refined the same family \citep{eegnex}. These
architectures work well within a session and are the standard baselines in the
field \citep{roy_review,craik}. Their limitation for our setting is that the
learned spatial filters are fixed at training time, so a montage or impedance
change between sessions is not compensated at all. We respond by placing an
adaptive whitening stage before the spatial filters, so that what the filters
see is referred to a covariance estimated on the current recording
(Section~\ref{sec:align}).

\subsection{Attention-based decoders}

EEG Conformer pairs a ShallowFBCSP-style stem with a
transformer encoder \citep{conformer}, and EEG-TCNet applies temporal
convolutions to the same problem \citep{eegtcnet}. The stem-plus-transformer combination is already published: an independent
implementation of it in our pipeline reproduced EEG Conformer's score without improving on it ($0.848$ against $0.843$), and we therefore claim no
novelty for that pairing. What these models share with the convolutional family
is a fixed front end, which is the property the present work targets.

\subsection{Alignment and transfer in BCI}

Riemannian methods treat the spatial covariance as a point on the SPD manifold
and classify with geodesic distances \citep{barachant,barachant2013,congedo}.
For transfer, Euclidean Alignment whitens each subject or session by
$\mathbf{M}^{-1/2}$ where $\mathbf{M}$ is its mean covariance \citep{hewu};
Riemannian Procrustes analysis adds rotation and scaling \citep{rodrigues}, and
parallel transport moves covariances between tangent spaces \citep{yair,zanini}.
These methods are effective and label-free, and reviews report them as the
strongest general-purpose transfer tools for EEG \citep{lotte2018,wu_transfer}.
Their limitation is operational. The alignment statistic
is computed over a whole recording, so the recording must be complete before
decoding starts, and within-recording drift is not tracked. We respond by moving
the estimator inside the network as a layer that updates online, and we then
show what that move costs (Section~\ref{sec:rate}).

\subsection{Riemannian and tangent-space representations}
\label{sec:rw_riem}

Spatial covariances are symmetric positive-definite matrices and do not form a vector space, so the methods built on them operate on the SPD manifold. The standard recipe computes a covariance per window, maps it to the tangent space at a reference point, and classifies the resulting vector with a linear model \citep{barachant,barachant2013,congedo}. The reference is usually the Riemannian or arithmetic mean of the training covariances, and the choice matters because the map is only a faithful local approximation near that point. These representations remain competitive with convolutional decoders on movement-intent tasks and are the strongest general-purpose transfer tools reported in reviews \citep{lotte2018,wu_transfer}. SPD networks learn the manifold operations instead of fixing them \citep{spdnet}.

Two differences separate the branch used here from that line. The tangent map is a second input to a convolutional decoder rather than the representation the decoder is built on, so first-order band power and second-order structure are read together instead of one replacing the other; Section~\ref{sec:fivecohort} reports that the two are complementary and that the branch alone is weaker than the stem alone. And the reference point is estimated from the incoming windows at inference rather than fixed after fitting, which is what makes the representation track a changing session and is also, as Section~\ref{sec:rate} shows, what makes it fail under a blocked protocol.

\subsection{Test-time adaptation}

Updating normalisation statistics on the test distribution improves accuracy
under image corruption in vision \citep{schneider,nado}, and Tent extends this to
updating affine parameters by entropy minimisation \citep{tent}. Test-time
training adds a self-supervised objective at inference \citep{ttt}. A recurring
finding in this literature is that these methods assume the test stream is
i.i.d., and degrade when it is not: NOTE addresses temporally correlated streams
by maintaining a class-balanced reservoir \citep{note}, SAR stabilises
adaptation under distribution shift with small or imbalanced batches
\citep{sar}, and CoTTA and LAME address error accumulation in continual
adaptation \citep{cotta,lame}. Our contribution relative to this line is
quantitative: we show that in a blocked EEG protocol the
relevant comparison is between the estimator's effective memory and the
duration of a single-class block, and that this comparison predicted a new
cohort in advance. The constraint should also have an upper side, because the
same estimator is what tracks the drift the layer exists to remove; that side
is not measured here (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Calibration and selective prediction}

Modern networks are overconfident and expected calibration error \citep{naeini,guo} degrades further under distribution shift \citep{ovadia}, which is the regime a longitudinally deployed decoder operates in. Selective classification allows a model to abstain \citep{chow,elyaniv}, and SelectiveNet trains the classifier and the selection function jointly under a coverage constraint \citep{selectivenet,geifman2017}. A device that must not move a wearer's legs on weak evidence is an obvious candidate for a reject option, so we built one in and tested it. It did not work: the trained selection function ranked windows no better than the classifier's own confidence, and the head is not part of the reported model. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} gives the measurement. We claim no contribution to selective prediction and report calibration error throughout because it is what a controller would threshold on.

\subsection{EEG correlates of gait and exoskeleton control}

Walking modulates cortical activity in a gait-phase-locked manner
\citep{gwin,seeber}, and treadmill walking can be decoded from scalp EEG
\citep{presacco,luu}. Robot-assisted gait modulates midline sensorimotor rhythms
\citep{wagner}. The intent signal our decoder targets is event-related
desynchronisation in the mu and beta bands, a power decrease over sensorimotor
cortex preceding and accompanying movement \citep{erd,pfurtscheller2006}. This
motivates a representation in which log-power ratios are preserved
(Section~\ref{sec:stem}), because ERD is defined as a relative power change.

\section{Problem formulation}

Let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times T}$ be a window of $C$ channels and $T$
samples with label $y \in \{\text{Stop}, \text{Walk}\}$. A recording session
$s$ supplies windows drawn from $p_s(\mathbf{X}, y)$. We assume the conditional
$p(y \mid \mathbf{X})$ is stable across sessions but the marginal
$p_s(\mathbf{X})$ is not, which is the covariate-shift assumption standard in
this literature \citep{sugiyama,shenoy}. Concretely we take the shift to act
primarily on the spatial covariance
$\mathbf{\Sigma}_s = \mathbb{E}_{p_s}[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top] / T$.

The decoder is fitted on sessions $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{fit}}$ and evaluated on
disjoint later sessions $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{test}}$, with no labels available
from $\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{test}}$ at any point. This is the constraint that
rules out per-session recalibration and motivates a label-free correction.

We quantify session drift by the affine-invariant Riemannian distance between
SPD matrices,
\begin{equation}
\delta(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}) =
\left\| \log\!\left( \mathbf{A}^{-1/2}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}^{-1/2} \right)
\right\|_F ,
\label{eq:riem}
\end{equation}
in preference to the Frobenius distance, because covariances do not form a vector
space and the Frobenius distance is dominated by overall scale, which would
flatter any whitening operation \citep{congedo,barachant}.

Two timescales govern the analysis in Section~\ref{sec:rate}. Let
$\tau_{\mathrm{blk}}$ be the median duration, in windows, of a contiguous
single-class block in the recording protocol, and let $\tau_{\mathrm{drift}}$ be
the timescale over which $\mathbf{\Sigma}_s$ changes appreciably. Both are
properties of the experimental design and the recording hardware, independent of the
model.

\subsection{Adaptive alignment}
\label{sec:align}

The layer maintains a running mean spatial covariance $\mathbf{M}$, applies
$\mathbf{M}^{-1/2}$, and interpolates between the aligned and the raw signal
through a learned scalar gate $g \in [0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}' = g \, \mathbf{M}^{-1/2} \mathbf{X} + (1-g)\, \mathbf{X} .
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}

Each window's covariance is normalised by its own trace before being
accumulated, so that the estimate is an average of shapes instead of an
average weighted by power:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M} \leftarrow (1-m)\,\mathbf{M} + \frac{m}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N}
\frac{\mathbf{C}_n}{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{C}_n)/C},
\qquad
\mathbf{C}_n = \frac{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_n \tilde{\mathbf{X}}_n^{\top}}{T},
\label{eq:update}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_n$ is the mean-centred window, $N$ the batch size and
$m$ the momentum. Without the trace normalisation the estimate is
power-weighted. On a cohort whose majority class carries $1.52\times$ the power
of the minority class, the un-normalised estimate sits at Riemannian distance
$0.306$ from the majority-class covariance, that is, it is the majority class;
the normalised estimate sits at $3.899$ and $3.194$ from the two class
covariances, between them. Section~\ref{sec:rejected} reports what correcting
this did and did not achieve.

Two properties separate this layer from batch normalisation \citep{batchnorm}.
The update in (\ref{eq:update}) uses only the covariance of incoming windows and
therefore runs on unlabelled test data. And the estimate continues to update in
evaluation mode, whereas BatchNorm freezes its running statistics at test time.
Freezing is correct when train and test share a distribution; here they are
weeks apart, and freezing discards the adaptation the layer exists to provide.

\paragraph{Propagation of the aligned signal} A whitening stage placed
before a normalisation layer invites the objection that the normalisation simply
undoes it. With identical initialisation on held-out windows, stem outputs
computed with and without alignment differ by $36\,\%$ in relative magnitude and
their correlation structure differs by $0.108$, so the transform is not absorbed
downstream.

\subsection{Multi-scale power stem}
\label{sec:stem}

Three parallel temporal resolutions (64, 128 and 256\,ms), each with its own
depthwise spatial filters, are followed by
$\mathrm{square} \rightarrow \mathrm{pool} \rightarrow \log$, after
\citet{schirrmeister}. Amplitude normalisation precedes the squaring so that
log-power ratios survive: scaling a window by $s$ shifts its log-power by
$2\log s$, a constant that a later affine layer absorbs, whereas normalising
after the square destroys the ratio in which ERD is defined \citep{erd}.

This ordering is load-bearing, and the evidence is a
dose--response across representations that differ in how much marginal power
they carry (Table~\ref{tab:repr}). Removing per-window amplitude normalisation
is worth $+0.134$ and $+0.174$ on band-power features, which are pure marginal
power; $-0.016$ and $+0.029$ on tangent-space features, which carry power only
through the covariance diagonal; and $-0.004$ and $+0.000$ on a correlation-only
representation, which is scale-free by construction and therefore cannot respond.
A representation that carries no marginal power shows no effect, which is what
identifies the mechanism instead of merely demonstrating it.

These are participant-paired comparisons, so they can be tested as such.
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests over participants, Holm-corrected across the three
representations within each cohort, give band power $+0.174$ (better in 7 of 8,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.047$) on cohort B and $+0.134$ (6 of 7,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.094$) on cohort A; tangent space $+0.029$ (5 of 8,
$p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.500$) and $-0.016$ (2 of 7, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.750$);
and the correlation-only control $+0.000$ (3 of 8, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=1.000$)
and $-0.004$ (3 of 7, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.750$). \textbf{The effect reaches
significance on the larger cohort and is null in the scale-free control, as
the mechanism predicts}; cohort A is consistent in sign but underpowered at
$n=7$, where the smallest attainable Holm-corrected $p$ is $0.047$.

A separate sweep over where normalisation is applied in the network confirms the
ordering at the architecture level: on cohort A with 4\,s windows, per-window
normalisation scores $0.849$, global per-recording normalisation $0.878$, and no
normalisation $0.864$, so the global setting used here is the best of the three
and per-window normalisation is the worst.

Table~\ref{tab:norm} repeats the normalisation comparison across six
published decoders, in an earlier training pipeline. Per-window normalisation is
the best choice for two of them and the worst for two, and most differences are
below the $0.02$ noise threshold, so this comparison neither supports nor
contradicts the representation-level dose--response in Table~\ref{tab:repr}.
Table~\ref{tab:repr} also reports the control that retired an amplitude
side-channel: supplying a per-window amplitude feature and supplying the same
feature with its window assignment shuffled differ by less than $0.02$ for four
of five models.

\subsection{Tangent-space branch}
\label{sec:tangent}

The stem of Section~\ref{sec:stem} reduces each window to log power per channel, which is the diagonal of its spatial covariance. The off-diagonal structure, how channels covary, is discarded. The branch restores it. For a window $X$ we form the per-window covariance $C = XX^{\top}/T$, normalise its trace, shrink it toward a scaled identity by $\lambda = 0.1$, and map it to the tangent space at a reference $M$,
\begin{equation}
\phi(X) = \mathrm{vec}\big(\log(M^{-1/2} C M^{-1/2})\big),
\end{equation}
with off-diagonal entries scaled by $\sqrt{2}$ so that the vector's Euclidean norm equals the matrix Frobenius norm. A linear projection maps $\phi(X)$ to the embedding width and concatenates it with the stem before the classifier, which leaves the classifier and the selective head unchanged in shape so the branch ablates as one module.

The reference is the running covariance the alignment layer of Section~\ref{sec:align} already maintains, updated without labels and still updating at test time. Where alignment is disabled the branch keeps its own running mean under the same momentum, so removing the branch does not silently remove adaptation as well. Tying the two mechanisms to one statistic is what places the branch under Condition~\ref{prop:band}: the rate that makes the alignment layer safe is the rate that makes the reference safe, and Section~\ref{sec:tanres} tests that consequence.

The tangent map carries no gradient; only the projection is learned. Backward through an eigendecomposition contains $1/(\lambda_i - \lambda_j)$ terms that diverge on near-degenerate spectra, and covariances estimated from 60 channels and 400 samples are routinely near-degenerate. Nothing measured is lost by detaching it: the probe that motivated the branch was itself a linear map on fixed tangent features, and that is what is trained here, jointly with the network rather than after it. The branch adds 270{,}924 parameters to the 20{,}019 of the stem.

\subsection{Causal cross-epoch context}

A causal transformer encoder \citep{attention} over a sequence of $K=8$ epochs
at stride 3 (a 14.5\,s span), following the epoch-sequence formulation standard
in sleep staging \citep{seqsleepnet,xsleepnet,deepsleepnet}. All masks are
causal, so only present and past information is used, which is the only variant
a worn device can run. Measured state persistence in cohort A supports a
sequence model: the self-transition probability is $0.984$ for Stop and $0.955$
for Walk at a $0.5$\,s step, corresponding to dwell times of 12 to 32\,s.

\subsection{Selective head}

A classifier paired with a selection function trained under a coverage
constraint \citep{selectivenet}, with a full-coverage auxiliary term so that the
backbone continues to learn from every sample, including the declined
region. We use the formulation as published.

\section{Experimental setup}

\subsection{Data}

\textbf{Cohort A (exoskeleton, OpenNeuro ds007788).} Seven participants, nine
sessions each, recorded across weeks while walking in a powered lower-limb
exoskeleton \citep{contrerasvidal}. Sixty-channel active EEG at 100\,Hz;
walk/stop labels are taken from the exoskeleton controller state. Windows of
4\,s at 0.5\,s stride give approximately 5031 fitting windows per participant.
The model is fitted on sessions 1--3 and evaluated on sessions 4--9.

\textbf{Cohort B (treadmill MoBI).} Eight participants, three trials each,
64-channel EEG at 100\,Hz, walk/stand from treadmill phase \citep{luu}. Fitted
on trial 1 and evaluated on trials 2--3, with 2\,s windows. The cohort is from
an independent laboratory with different sensors, a different task framing, and
reversed class balance: Stop is the minority class at approximately $12\,\%$
here and the majority class in cohort A.

\textbf{Cohort C (motor execution, PhysioNet EEGMMIDB).} Twenty participants,
S001--S020, from the PhysioNet motor movement and imagery database
\citep{schalk2004,goldberger2000}, recorded with 64-channel EEG at 160\,Hz and
resampled to 100\,Hz. Only the motor-execution runs are used, and the task is
rest against movement of the hands or feet. The model is fitted on runs 3, 5,
7 and 9 and evaluated on runs 11 and 13, with 2\,s windows at 0.5\,s stride,
each lying wholly inside one annotated period. The protocol alternates the two
classes about every 4\,s, a median of 5 windows per class block. Its analysis
plan was committed to version control before any model was trained on it
(Section~\ref{sec:prereg}).

The protocols differ sharply in temporal structure, which
Section~\ref{sec:rate} shows to be decisive (Table~\ref{tab:blocks}).

\subsection{Preprocessing and training}

Signals are band-pass filtered to 8--30\,Hz and resampled to 100\,Hz. Cohort A
uses a zero-phase FIR filter in MNE-Python \citep{gramfort}, after which the
EOG channels, filtered to the same band, are regressed linearly out of the EEG.
Cohorts B and C use a zero-phase fourth-order Butterworth filter and have no EOG step. No other artefact rejection, ICA decomposition or channel interpolation
is applied, so that the alignment layer is evaluated on the shift it is meant
to correct. The band also excludes the gait cycle, near 1--2\,Hz, and most
electromyographic power above 30\,Hz. Amplitude
normalisation is applied globally per recording, before the squaring stage, for
the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:stem}.

Table~\ref{tab:hyper} lists the training configuration. The adaptation momentum
is $m=0.2$ unless stated otherwise; Section~\ref{sec:rate} treats $m$ as the
object of study. Model selection uses balanced accuracy on a held-out split of
the fitting sessions, evaluated at the best epoch by that criterion, which is
conventional but upward-biased on small folds (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Outcome measures}

Balanced accuracy for comparability with the literature; expected calibration
error (ECE) with 10 equal-width bins \citep{naeini}, because a device acting on
a confidence value needs that value to mean something; accuracy at $90\,\%$
coverage, the trained selective operating point; spurious activations per minute
of standing; and detection latency, reported alongside the benefit it buys.

\paragraph{Leakage guard} Unconditioned nuisance-decodability is confounded with
accuracy. In our pipeline a decoder that uses no movement information whatever
measures $R^2 = +0.863$ against the movement signal, because both the decoder
output and the nuisance are driven by the class label. Movement leakage is
therefore measured conditional on the class label throughout, by centring the
nuisance features within class before regression.

We calibrate the probe by injecting the recorded movement signal into the EEG,
spatially mixed with a fixed per-participant gain matrix, at a known fraction
$f$ of signal power. The calibration uses the tangent-space representation.
Accuracy rises monotonically with $f$: $0.763$ at $f=0$, then $0.819$, $0.873$,
$0.892$ and $0.913$ at $f = 0.001$, $0.005$, $0.01$ and $0.05$. The conditional
probe rises with it, from $-0.112$ at $f=0$ through $+0.015$ at $f=0.005$ to
$+0.067$ at $f=0.02$, where accuracy is $0.903$. Reaching an accuracy near
$0.90$ through injected movement therefore moves the probe to about $+0.07$.
The proposed \textit{align + gate} decoder reaches $0.901$ with a conditional
probe of $-0.068$, and all eight cohort-A arms lie between $-0.066$ and
$-0.082$, on the uncontaminated side of the calibration curve. Two limits
apply. The calibration was performed on a different representation from the
decoder it is compared with, and the conditional probe cannot register artefact
information that is constant within each class. The comparison is therefore
evidence against movement-driven accuracy. Section~\ref{sec:artefact} adds a
second, independent test by removing artefact components and retraining.

\subsection{Statistical treatment and measurement noise}
\label{sec:noise}

Drift reductions are tested with a two-sided paired $t$-test over participants,
pairing each participant's aligned distance against its raw distance. Accuracy
comparisons are reported as the mean over participants with the between-subject
standard deviation, and as paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests over participants
wherever per-participant results were retained (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
Every $\pm$ in a table is labelled in its caption as across participants or
across data-split seeds. Standard deviations across participants are population
values, and standard deviations across seeds are sample values.

Two ablation arms disable alignment entirely and therefore never invoke the
running estimator. For them the momentum and the estimator version are inert, so
every run is a true repeat and they measure the noise floor directly
(Table~\ref{tab:noise}).

The stem-only arm reproduces $0.7375$ bit-for-bit across three runs; the arms
containing the cross-epoch transformer do not, and the attention path is the
only structural difference between them. We attribute the variation to
non-deterministic reductions in scaled dot-product attention on GPU.

Run-to-run variation at a fixed split seed is a lower bound on the relevant
noise. Across three data-split seeds the proposed configuration spans $0.839$ to
$0.884$, a range of $0.046$ and a standard deviation of $0.026$
(Table~\ref{tab:noise}), several times the fixed-seed figure. This is the
quantity that governs whether two models can be ranked, and differences between
models smaller than it are reported without a ranking. The spread also
differs between arms. On cohort B at a memory of 320 windows it is $0.003$ for
\textit{align + gate} and $0.037$ for the no-align arm, which contains the
transformer, so single-seed values of the no-align arm on that cohort lie inside
its own seed spread.

Any arm containing the transformer carries approximately $0.010$ run-to-run
noise at a fixed seed, so differences below $0.02$ are not interpreted anywhere
in this paper. The effects we do interpret on accuracy are measured over three data-split
seeds and tested paired within participant (Tables~\ref{tab:compare} and
\ref{tab:ratecurve}, Section~\ref{sec:twosided}). An exact null in the deterministic
arm is stronger evidence than one merely within noise, because that arm has no
noise to hide in.

\paragraph{Pipeline separation} The proposed decoder and the published decoders
of Section~\ref{sec:compare} were trained in one pipeline.
Tables~\ref{tab:repr} and \ref{tab:norm} come from earlier
pipelines and are used only for comparisons within each table.

\subsection{The drift mechanism}
\label{sec:mechanism}

We measure the Riemannian distance (\ref{eq:riem}) between the fitting-session
mean covariance and each held-out session's, before and after the layer
(Fig.~\ref{fig:drift}, Table~\ref{tab:drift}).

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../results/fig_drift.pdf}
\caption{Session-drift reduction per participant. Bars are the Riemannian
distance $\delta$ between the fitting-session mean covariance and the held-out
session mean, for the raw signal, the momentum-0 no-op control, and the aligned
signal. (a) cohort A, $n=7$; (b) cohort B, $n=8$. Distance falls in every
participant of both cohorts; the no-op control, which shares every code path but
never updates its estimate, leaves the distance unchanged.}
\label{fig:drift}
\end{figure*}

Drift is reduced in 15 of 15 participants ($t=-9.46$, $p=8\times10^{-5}$ for
cohort A; $t=-12.33$, $p=5\times10^{-6}$ for cohort B), with per-participant
reductions spanning $38.7$ to $51.2\,\%$ on cohort A and $43.8$ to $82.1\,\%$ on
cohort B (Table~\ref{tab:drift}). No participant regresses, and the
reduction is not carried by one or two outliers. The no-op control leaves
the distance unchanged, which establishes that the effect comes from adaptation
and excludes both the whitening operation and the measurement itself.

The no-op control runs at momentum $0$. Its running estimate is primed by
copying the first batch's covariance and never updates, so it applies one fixed
linear map to every session, $M = gW + (1-g)I$ with $g=\sigma(2.0)\approx0.88$.
A fixed map applied to both covariances is a congruence, under which the
affine-invariant distance (\ref{eq:riem}) does not change, and the control
accordingly changes the distance by less than $0.1\,\%$ of its raw value in
every participant (Table~\ref{tab:drift}). An earlier version of this
measurement normalised each covariance by its trace after the map, which breaks
that invariance and made the control scatter in both directions; it was
removed, and the values reported here replace the larger reductions it produced
($52.0$ and $83.9\,\%$).

The mechanism works as designed, and Section~\ref{sec:external} shows
that this does not settle whether it helps the task.

The acc@90 value for the \textit{align + ctx} arm is withheld because with the
selection head disabled the model emits near-constant confidence, so ranking by
it selects the first $90\,\%$ of the test set in recording order. That number

\paragraph{Selective prediction} Abstention is supposed to
raise accuracy on the windows that are kept. It does not. Across the 18 arms in
this work for which both quantities were computed on the same windows with the
same metric, declining the least-confident $10\,\%$ \textbf{lowers} balanced
accuracy in 14, by $0.045$ on average; the headline arm loses $0.026$
($0.901 \rightarrow 0.876$) and the worst case loses $0.122$. The four arms
where abstention helps are exactly those with no trained gate
(\textit{align + ctx}, \textit{stem only}), where the ranking is arbitrary.
A gate that is beaten by its own absence is not ranking windows by correctness,
and the selective head is therefore reported as a component for which no benefit was demonstrated. Fixing it, for instance by thresholding a
temperature-scaled softmax confidence instead of the learned gate, is left
open. Calibration is compared with the published decoders in
Section~\ref{sec:compare}.

The head contributes no accuracy either. Under the trace-normalised estimator, an \textit{align}-only arm reaches $0.883$ against $0.884$ with the head, a difference of $0.002$, and adding the head to \textit{align + ctx} changes accuracy by $0.000$ ($0.862$ in both arms). Calibration does not suffer either: without the head $\mathrm{ECE}$ is $0.038$, against $0.042$ with it, both under the same estimator. Over three data-split seeds the \textit{align}-only arm averages $0.869$ against $0.868$ with the head, a difference of $-0.001$ (higher with the head in 4 of 7 participants, Wilcoxon $p=1.00$), and mean $\mathrm{ECE}$ is $0.054$ without the head against $0.058$ with it. An earlier difference of $+0.016$ between the same two context arms under the original estimator is $1.6\times$ the $0.010$ noise floor of arms containing the transformer (Table~\ref{tab:noise}), and it does not reappear in the comparison made with one estimator throughout. On this evidence the selective head could be removed with no loss of accuracy or calibration. It is kept in the arms reported here so that they remain comparable with the ablation.

\paragraph{Dependence on the alignment stage} With alignment removed, the
context and selection modules together contribute between $0.000$ and $0.007$:
the no-align arm spans $0.827$--$0.834$ across repeats (Table~\ref{tab:noise})
while the stem-only arm is deterministic at $0.827$. Against the $0.010$ noise floor that is indistinguishable from nothing, and well below alignment's own contribution: $+0.051$ under the original estimator
($0.878$ against $0.827$, Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) and $+0.028$ under the
trace-normalised one ($0.862$ against $0.834$) from the estimator-fix run of
Section~\ref{sec:rejected}. The lower figure should be read with care: the
no-align arm is inert to the estimator, so its $0.834$ differs from $0.827$
only by run-to-run variation, and part of the gap between the two figures is that noise. The comparison that isolates alignment most directly, \textit{align + gate} against \textit{gate} with nothing else changed, was repeated over three data-split seeds under the trace-normalised estimator. With each participant's accuracy averaged over seeds, alignment raises accuracy by $+0.026$ ($0.868$ against $0.842$), higher in 6 of 7 participants (Wilcoxon $p=0.22$). The effect is $+0.067$, $+0.029$ and $-0.019$ on the three seeds, and it is uneven across participants: sub-04 gains $0.179$ and sub-05 loses $0.127$, each with the same sign on every seed. The gain from alignment on cohort A is therefore close to the trace-normalised single-seed figure of $+0.028$, and with seven participants it is not significant.
Table~\ref{tab:ablation3} removes the single-seed ordering as well. Over the two
seeds every arm shares, the stem-only arm leads: every other arm lies $0.019$ to
$0.027$ below it paired over those seeds and is higher in at most 3 of 7
participants, and no difference is distinguishable from zero (Holm-corrected
$p \geq 0.94$). Over three seeds the stem-only arm averages $0.864 \pm 0.032$,
level with the $0.868$ of \textit{align + gate}, because its seed 0 sits at
$0.827$ against $0.881$ and $0.884$ on the other two. The ranking between these
arms is therefore set by the seed, not by the components. Against the full configuration the stem's lead
is carried by two participants (sub-05 $+0.137$, sub-01 $+0.075$) and reversed
by a third (sub-04 $-0.130$). The alignment arms also average more spurious
activations per minute than the corresponding arms without alignment ($1.60$
against $0.89$ over three seeds with the gate, and $1.17$ against $0.74$ over
seeds 1 and 2 with context and gate), yet they are higher in only 3 of 7
participants ($p=0.44$ and $p=0.81$). On cohort A, therefore, neither alignment
nor the other modules are shown to raise accuracy over the stem, and none is
shown to lower it. The measured effect of alignment on this cohort is the
reduction in session drift (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).

\paragraph{Cross-epoch context} Over three seeds under the current estimator
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation3}), adding it changes accuracy from $0.868$ to $0.859$, a
difference below the $0.02$ this paper interprets for arms containing the
transformer, raises ECE from $0.058$ to $0.072$, and reduces spurious activations from
$1.60$ to $1.16$ per minute, a $27\,\%$ reduction. Paired tests are not
available because seed 0 of the context arm did not retain per-participant
rows. It is therefore reported as a selectable safety mode, outside the default configuration. Removing it also removes 594{,}816 of the model's 618{,}997
parameters, which is what makes the default configuration compact.

\subsection{Artefact control}
\label{sec:artefact}

Walk and stop differ in movement, so accuracy on this task could arise from
muscle or motion artefact. The proposed configuration was therefore retrained
after independent component analysis, with both arms restricted to the training
task so that they differ only in the cleaning step. Each recording was
re-referenced to the average, a copy band-passed to 1--45\,Hz was decomposed
into 30 components with the extended Picard algorithm \citep{picard}, and the
components that ICLabel \citep{iclabel} classified as eye, muscle, heart, line
noise or channel noise were removed before band-passing to the decoding band.
Across the 63 recordings, a mean of 6.8 of 30 components was removed, most often
eye (42\,\% of removals) and muscle (38\,\%). Gait-related artefact is difficult
to separate from cortical activity during walking \citep{castermans2014,kline2015},
and ICLabel is not trained on it specifically, so this control narrows the
question and leaves part of it open.

\subsection{Comparison with published decoders}
\label{sec:compare}

Table~\ref{tab:compare} compares the proposed configuration with the eight
published decoders of the experimental setup, all trained in the same pipeline,
on the three cohorts.

On cohort A, over the same three data-split seeds, the proposed configuration
averages $0.868$ and the published decoders average $0.817$ to $0.878$. Paired over
participants with seeds averaged, no difference is significant in either
direction. EEG-TCNet and EEGNeX lie $0.010$ and $0.008$ above it (higher in 4 and 3
of 7 participants; Wilcoxon $p=0.58$ and $p=0.94$), and the other six lie
$0.003$ to $0.051$ below it (smallest $p=0.22$). At the first seed alone the proposed
configuration is the highest of the nine ($0.884$ against $0.823$ to $0.871$),
so single-seed orderings on this cohort are not stable. It holds no parameter
advantage over the smallest published decoders: EEG-TCNet averages $0.878$ with
7{,}062 parameters and FBLightConvNet $0.866$ with 23{,}760, against 24{,}181.

On cohort C, over three seeds, the proposed configuration averages $0.796$, and
the published decoders that trained reach $0.723$ to $0.783$. Paired
over participants with seeds averaged, it is higher than EEGNeX by $0.013$
(higher in 15 of 20 participants; Wilcoxon $p=0.17$), than EEG Conformer by
$0.013$ (12 of 20; $p=0.15$), than FBLightConvNet by $0.018$ (15 of 20;
$p=0.10$), than ShallowFBCSPNet by $0.031$ (16 of 20; $p=0.011$) and than
TSception by $0.073$ (19 of 20; $p<0.001$). After Holm correction across these
five comparisons, only the differences from ShallowFBCSPNet ($p=0.044$) and
TSception ($p<0.001$) remain below $0.05$. Its expected calibration
error is $0.061$, against $0.077$ to $0.083$ for the same decoders.
EEGNet, Deep4Net and EEG-TCNet did not train reliably on this cohort under the
shared settings: on each seed they stay near chance ($<0.55$) for 6 to 9, 10 to
12 and 7 to 11 of the twenty participants respectively, so their cohort C values
record a training failure. The low calibration error of EEGNet and EEG-TCNet
there ($0.059$ and $0.054$) shows why
calibration error is read together with accuracy, since a decoder near chance
that reports low confidence is well calibrated.

On cohort B the lower bound of Condition~\ref{prop:band} disables alignment,
and the configuration without it averages $0.729$ over three seeds, against $0.617$
to $0.734$ for the published decoders. Paired over participants with seeds
averaged, it is higher than seven of them, by $0.013$ to $0.111$, and $0.005$ below
TSception (higher in 4 of 8 participants; Wilcoxon $p=0.84$). After Holm
correction across the eight comparisons no difference remains below $0.05$.
With alignment enabled at the default rate the configuration averages $0.580$,
below every published decoder, which is the loss the lower bound exists to
prevent. Without alignment its calibration error is $0.066$, against $0.066$ to $0.211$
for the published decoders.

On cohort A the proposed configuration's calibration error averages $0.058$
across seeds, against $0.058$ to $0.076$ for the published decoders over the same
seeds. Its accuracy is therefore level with the published decoders on cohort
A, slightly higher on cohort C and level on cohort B once alignment is disabled
there, without a significant difference against the strongest of them on any
cohort, and its calibration error is lower on cohort C. What the
architecture adds at this accuracy is an alignment layer that updates without
labels at test time, a measured reduction of session drift
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}), and a rule, checkable before training, for when
that layer must be slowed (Section~\ref{sec:rate}).

($0.901$ against $0.827$) and $-0.149$ on cohort B over three seeds against the gate-only arm ($0.580$ against $0.729$;
lower in 8 of 8 participants, Wilcoxon $p=0.008$). The cohort A figure comes from one seed and also removes the context module. Against the gate-only arm over three seeds, alignment is worth $+0.026$ there (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), so the size of the inversion comes mostly from the loss on cohort B. Over seeds 1 and 2 no arm differs from the stem alone on cohort A (Table~\ref{tab:ablation3}), so the inversion sets that loss against no measurable effect on cohort A.
The remainder of the results characterises this inversion.

\paragraph{A protocol-level switch} The two cohorts do not require
two architectures. The lower bound of Condition~\ref{prop:band} acts as a switch
whose position is fixed by the recording protocol: with the default estimator
memory $\mu = 32/m = 160$ windows, cohort A's $\tau_{\mathrm{blk}}=18$ satisfies
$\tau_{\mathrm{blk}} < \mu$ and cohort B's $\tau_{\mathrm{blk}}=214$ does not.
Enabling alignment exactly when the lower bound is satisfied selects $0.901$ on
cohort A and $0.792$ on cohort B, the best configuration of
Table~\ref{tab:external} on each at seed 0, from one model and one rule
evaluated before any training. Over seeds 1 and 2 the cohort A half of that
selection is unconfirmed, because the stem-only arm leads there without a
significant difference (Table~\ref{tab:ablation3}); what the switch is shown to
do is avoid the loss on cohort B.

\paragraph{Learnability of the switch} The architecture
already contains a learnable version of it: the blend weight
$g=\sigma(\mathrm{gate})$ in (\ref{eq:update}) is a trained parameter, so the
model is free to close the gate and bypass alignment. On cohort B it does not,
and scores $0.626$ at seed 0. The reason is structural and does not lie in optimisation. The gate is trained on the fitting sessions, where the estimator
sees interleaved windows and alignment is harmless; the class-tracking failure
appears only at test time, when the estimator meets contiguous blocks longer
than its memory. \textbf{No training signal contains the pathology, so no
gradient can close the gate against it.} This is the argument for a
protocol-level check instead of an end-to-end one: the quantity that decides
whether the layer helps is a property of the evaluation stream, which the
fitting distribution does not expose.

\subsection{Correcting the covariance estimator}
\label{sec:rejected}

One candidate explanation is the power-weighted estimator described in
Section~\ref{sec:align}. The defect is real and measurable: on cohort B, where
the majority class carries $1.52\times$ the power, the un-normalised running
estimate sat at Riemannian distance $0.306$ from the walk covariance, which is
to say it had become the majority class. The per-window trace normalisation in
(\ref{eq:update}) moves it to $3.899$ and $3.194$ from the two class
covariances.

Correcting it changed decoding by $+0.005$ on cohort B and $-0.016$ on cohort A.
The estimator improved and the task did not follow, so this is therefore not the explanation for the inversion, though the correction is retained, which is
independently right. It is the first of five cases in this
work where a distribution-level quantity improved without the task following
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{The adaptation rate condition}
\label{sec:rate}

The running estimate tracks whatever is currently
streaming. An exponential moving average with momentum $m$ has an effective
memory of approximately $1/m$ batches, or $NB/m$ windows at batch size $N$ and
$B$ batches. When a protocol presents a class in contiguous blocks longer than
that memory, the estimate converges to the currently streaming class, and
whitening by it removes the between-class difference. Cohort A's blocks
($\tau_{\mathrm{blk}}=18$) sit far inside even the fastest memory; cohort B's
($\tau_{\mathrm{blk}}=214$) do not.

Fig.~\ref{fig:tracking} shows the estimate itself for one participant from each
cohort. It is the layer's internal state, which depends only on the input
windows and the momentum and on no trained weight, so it is computed without
training. At $m=0.2$ the estimate on cohort A stays away from both class
covariances, while on cohort B it moves to the covariance of the class being
streamed and follows it through the long walk block. At $m=0.01$ the estimate
on cohort B still moves toward the walk covariance, more slowly, and crosses
over late in the recording.

If this mechanism is responsible, slowing adaptation until the memory exceeds a class block should restore cohort B. The memory is approximately 160, 640 and 3200 windows
at $m=0.2$, $0.05$ and $0.01$, so the crossing of
$\tau_{\mathrm{blk}}=214$ falls between the first and second settings.
Three seeds under the current estimator trace the recovery more finely for
\textit{align + gate} (Table~\ref{tab:ratecurve}), against the gate-only arm,
which differs only in alignment and does not use the estimator ($0.729$). Alignment
costs $0.149$ at a memory of 160 windows (lower in 8 of 8 participants; Wilcoxon
$p=0.008$), $0.091$ at 320 (7 of 8; $p=0.016$), $0.034$ at 1600 (6 of 8; $p=0.15$) and
$0.031$ at 3200 (6 of 8; $p=0.15$). The cost falls by similar amounts on either
side of the block length and then levels off, so the recovery is gradual and
shows no step at $\tau_{\mathrm{blk}}=214$. Alignment never becomes beneficial,
and at the two slowest rates its remaining cost is not significant.

At seed 0, every arm that uses alignment gains $+0.090$ to $+0.098$ between
memories of 160 and 640 windows (Table~\ref{tab:rate}). Neither arm that omits alignment
responds: the no-align arm moves $-0.010$, within its $0.0107$ run-to-run spread
(Table~\ref{tab:noise}), and the stem-only arm reproduces $0.7375$ exactly at
every setting despite a 160-fold change in the adaptation rate. Because that arm
is deterministic, this null is exact. The
manipulated variable therefore acts only through the component it controls,
which an unrelated confound such as optimisation dynamics or regularisation
would not produce.

\subsection{Slow adaptation has a cost}
\label{sec:twosided}

On cohort A, whose blocks sit far inside even the fastest memory so that the estimate is already class-mixed, the same change was expected to be neutral. This expectation was registered before the experiment was run. It is not borne out
(Table~\ref{tab:rate}).

The optima are opposite, and over three seeds error in either direction costs
accuracy: slowing costs $0.050$ on cohort A, and on cohort B the default rate
gives up $0.118$ against a memory of 3200 windows
(Table~\ref{tab:ratecurve}). Slower adaptation therefore costs accuracy on
cohort A. One reading is a trade-off in which a fast estimate tracks both the
streaming class, which is harmful, and session drift, which is beneficial. Measured without a decoder, slowing the estimate also stops it removing drift:
on cohort A the reduction in session-to-session distance falls from
$45.9\,\%$ at $m=0.2$ to $12.1\,\%$ at $m=0.05$ and $0.2\,\%$ at $m=0.01$, and on
cohort B from $62.5\,\%$ to $45.5\,\%$ and $11.1\,\%$. At
$m=0.01$ the estimator's memory, about 3200 windows, is ten times a held-out
session of about 313 windows, so this measures how far the estimate can move
within the data available and does not isolate a drift timescale
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

That cost was first measured on \textit{align + ctx + gate}, which is not the
configuration this paper proposes. Repeating it on the proposed
\textit{align + gate} arm gives a similar figure: slowing to
$m=0.01$ takes cohort A from $0.884$ to $0.799$ at the first data-split seed
under the trace-normalised estimator, a cost of $0.085$. Over three seeds the
slow arm averages $0.819 \pm 0.017$ against $0.868 \pm 0.026$, a cost of
$\mathbf{-0.050}$ with each participant's accuracy averaged over seeds, lower in
7 of 7 participants (Wilcoxon $p=0.016$, the smallest attainable with seven
participants). The cost is $-0.085$, $-0.056$ and $-0.008$ on the three seeds,
so the first-seed figure is the largest of the three. Both arms use the same
estimator version; comparing the slow arm against the original-estimator
$0.901$ would inflate the cost by mixing estimator versions.

\begin{condition}[Admissible rate band]
\label{prop:band}
Assume (i) the running estimate is an exponential moving average with effective
memory $\mu$ windows; (ii) the within-class covariance is stationary over a
block; (iii) the protocol presents classes in contiguous blocks of length
$\tau_{\mathrm{blk}}$; and (iv) session drift acts as a congruence transform
slow on the scale $\tau_{\mathrm{drift}}$. Under (i)--(iv) the estimate both
avoids tracking the streaming class and tracks session drift only if
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{blk}} \;<\; \mu \;<\; \tau_{\mathrm{drift}} .
\label{eq:band}
\end{equation}
Where $\tau_{\mathrm{blk}} \ge \tau_{\mathrm{drift}}$ the band is empty and no
setting of $\mu$ satisfies both requirements. It is a design condition. Its lower bound is tested here empirically, once in
advance; its upper bound is not measured, and we give no proof.
\end{condition}

Cohort A satisfies the lower bound with a wide margin ($\tau_{\mathrm{blk}}=18$
against $\mu=160$), and cohort B's 214-window blocks violate it at the default
rate. On cohort B the cost of alignment falls gradually as $\mu$ lengthens past
$\tau_{\mathrm{blk}}$ (Section~\ref{sec:rate}), so the bound marks where class
tracking begins to weaken, and it does not mark a sharp threshold. Whether cohort B has any admissible rate cannot be decided from these
data, because its drift timescale could not be measured
(Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\begin{remark}[The residual]
At the slowest rates measured, memories of 1600 and 3200 windows, alignment
still costs $0.034$ and $0.031$ on cohort B against the gate-only arm (lower in 6 of
8 participants at both; $p=0.15$), a residual that is not significant with eight
participants. Measured without a decoder on cohort B
itself, the estimate still removes $22.3\,\%$ and $11.1\,\%$ of session drift at
those rates, against $2.3\,\%$ and $0.2\,\%$ on cohort A, so the residual does not
come from an absence of drift correction: on cohort B the drift that the slow
estimate removes does not turn into accuracy. The residual is therefore
reported as unexplained, and it is a further case of a distribution-level
improvement without a task improvement (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Offline alignment is exempt]
A whole-recording estimate, as in Euclidean Alignment \citep{hewu}, is the
maximally slow setting. It therefore never tracks the class, and it never tracks
within-recording drift either, which is exactly the capability an in-network
version is introduced to add.
\end{remark}

The lower bound in (\ref{eq:band}) is a property of the recording protocol.
$\tau_{\mathrm{blk}}$ is read off the trial structure, so the bound can be
checked before a model is fitted, and Section~\ref{sec:prereg} tests it on a
cohort whose results were not available when the prediction was made. The upper
bound was not measured: two label-free estimates of $\tau_{\mathrm{drift}}$ were
attempted, and neither resolved it (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{A prediction registered before a third cohort}
\label{sec:prereg}

Condition~\ref{prop:band} was formulated after cohort~B failed, so on the two
cohorts above it is an explanation written with their results in view. To test
it in advance, predictions for a third cohort were committed to version control
before any model was trained on it. The cohort, participants, data split,
preprocessing, compared arms and decision thresholds were all fixed at that
point.

Cohort~C alternates rest and movement about every 4\,s. From the published
protocol alone a class block holds about five windows, and the loader later
measured a median of 5.0. Against an estimator memory of 160 windows at
$m=0.2$, the lower bound of (\ref{eq:band}) is satisfied by a factor of about
thirty, so the estimate cannot converge to either class. Two predictions
follow. P1: enabling alignment costs less than $0.05$, where the same comparison

$0.05$, where it recovered $0.090$ on cohort~B. The arms compared in P1 differ
only in alignment, and those in P2 only in momentum.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Validating a mechanism separately from performance}

Domain adaptation and invariance methods are routinely justified by a
distribution-level quantity, a divergence, an alignment distance or a
nuisance-decodability score, with task performance assumed to follow
\citep{ganin,long,coral,bendavid}. This work provides five counterexamples
within a single system, three of which we produced by acting on that assumption
ourselves (Fig.~\ref{fig:metrics}).

\begin{figure}[pos=htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../results/fig_metrics.pdf}
\caption{Five cases in which a distribution-level quantity improved (right, in
blue) and the task did not follow (left, in red). The final row has no accuracy
change and therefore no bar: the leakage probe rises with accuracy whether or
not the decoder can access the nuisance.}
\label{fig:metrics}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
\item The adaptation rate was originally selected because it maximised drift
      reduction (on cohort A, $45.9\,\%$ at $m=0.2$ against $12.1\,\%$ at $m=0.05$ and $0.2\,\%$ at $m=0.01$). That choice costs $0.118$ accuracy
      on cohort B against a memory of 3200 windows, over three seeds
      (Table~\ref{tab:ratecurve}).
\item The cohort in which more drift was removed ($62.5\,\%$ against $45.9\,\%$, Table~\ref{tab:drift}) is the
      cohort in which the layer costs $0.149$ accuracy over three seeds
      (Table~\ref{tab:ratecurve}).
\item A verified correction to a genuine covariance-estimation bias moved
      decoding by $+0.005$ and $-0.016$ (Section~\ref{sec:rejected}).
\item A prediction registered from our own diagnosis was falsified
      (Table~\ref{tab:rate}), because the diagnosis accounted only for the
      harm the estimate can do.
\item The nuisance-leakage probe rises with accuracy whether or not the decoder
      can access the nuisance, measuring $R^2=+0.863$ for one that cannot.
\end{enumerate}

Item 4 generalises the rest. Each of these quantities is one-sided by
construction: it scores the failure it was designed to detect and carries no
term for the capability the intervention removes. Drift reduction measures how
much non-stationarity a layer removes and has no term for what the layer
destroys. A diagnosis inherited from such a metric inherits its blind spot,
which is why our own threshold rule was correct about one direction and silent
about the other until we tested it where it predicted no effect. The practical
implication is that reporting a reduced divergence, increased invariance or
lowered nuisance decodability is evidence about the representation alone, saying nothing about whether the method helps, even when the reduction is large and
reproducible.

\subsection{Relation to temporally correlated test-time adaptation}

The vision literature has identified that test-time adaptation degrades when the
test stream is temporally correlated \citep{note,sar,cotta}, and proposes
reservoirs and stabilised objectives as remedies. Our result is compatible with
that line and adds two things specific to the blocked-protocol case. First, the
relevant quantity is a ratio of timescales, measured on the stream, and which side of the threshold a protocol falls on can be predicted in advance, as the third cohort shows (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}). Second, slowing adaptation is not free. On both cohorts with short blocks it
costs accuracy, so remedies that slow adaptation can exchange class tracking for
a different loss, which on these cohorts could not be attributed to drift
tracking directly.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Several limitations constrain the interpretation of these results.

\textbf{Seeds.} The component ablation of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and the rate tables are single-seed; Table~\ref{tab:ablation3} repeats the ablation over three seeds, two for the stem-only arm.
The proposed configuration was rerun over three data-split seeds under the
trace-normalised estimator, giving $0.868 \pm 0.026$ (seeds $0.884$, $0.882$ and
$0.839$). That seed standard deviation is larger than the $0.010$ noise floor of
repeated runs at a fixed split (Table~\ref{tab:noise}), and it is concentrated in
two participants, whose accuracy ranges over $0.217$ and $0.120$ across seeds
while the other five vary by at most $0.065$. The rate effects are therefore reported over three seeds with paired tests
(Table~\ref{tab:ratecurve}, Section~\ref{sec:twosided}). The paired alignment effect on cohort A over the same three seeds is
$+0.026$ ($p=0.22$) and is negative on one seed, so the benefit of alignment
where the lower bound holds is small and unconfirmed at this sample size, and
over seeds 1 and 2 the stem-only arm leads every arm on the mean without a
significant paired difference (Table~\ref{tab:ablation3}). The
loss where it fails is $0.149$ on cohort B over three seeds (lower in 8 of 8
participants; $p=0.008$). The published decoders were run over the same three seeds on cohorts A and C.

\textbf{No paired significance tests on accuracy.} Per-participant accuracies were not retained for the single-seed component ablation and the single-seed rate tables, so those comparisons carry dispersion without paired tests. Paired tests are reported wherever per-participant rows exist: the drift results (Table~\ref{tab:drift}), the temporal and normalisation controls, the artefact control (Section~\ref{sec:artefact}), the three-seed alignment comparison and the two-seed contrasts against the stem on cohort A (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}, Table~\ref{tab:ablation3}),
the comparison with published decoders on cohort~C (Section~\ref{sec:compare})
and the cohort~C predictions (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}).

\textbf{Cohort size.} Seven, eight and twenty participants. This is typical for
exoskeleton BCI studies \citep{he_exo} but small, and the between-subject SD of
$0.06$ to $0.13$ (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) is large relative to the differences
between arms.

\textbf{Best-epoch evaluation.} Model selection uses the best epoch by held-out
balanced accuracy, which is conventional but upward-biased on small folds. The
bias applies equally to all arms and baselines within a pipeline, so comparisons
within a pipeline are unaffected, but absolute values should be read as
optimistic.

\textbf{Baseline training.} The published decoders were trained with the proposed
decoder's settings and no per-model tuning. EEGNet, Deep4Net and EEG-TCNet stayed near chance for several cohort~C participants under those settings, so their cohort~C
results record a training failure and are not evidence about those
architectures.

\textbf{The upper bound is not measured.} Two label-free estimates of
$\tau_{\mathrm{drift}}$ were attempted. Block-wise covariance distances within a
session are dominated by class alternation where blocks are short, and on
cohort~A they return about 18 windows, the class-block length. Distances between
session mean covariances are already at their plateau at a gap of one session
for every cohort~A participant, and fall with the gap for two, so a fitted
timescale lies below the sampling interval and is not a measurement; cohort~B's
three trials give two gaps, too few for a fit. The data therefore bound the
drift timescale only from above, at about one session, which does not constrain
the adaptation rate. A protocol with more closely spaced recordings is needed to
test the upper bound.

\textbf{Condition~\ref{prop:band} is hypothesis-generating.} It was formulated after observing cohort B fail. One prediction from it has since been tested
out-of-sample, and that test falsified the one-sided form
(Table~\ref{tab:rate}), which is how the two-sided form arose. The corrected form has since been tested prospectively on cohort~C (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}), where both registered predictions held. That test covers the lower bound and the contrast with cohort~B; the upper bound has not been tested in advance.

\textbf{Artefact.} Retraining after ICA cleaning lowers accuracy by $0.051$, concentrated in three participants (Section~\ref{sec:artefact}). The losses do not follow the amount of muscle activity removed. With seven participants, and with re-referencing part of the cleaning, the control still falls short of establishing that the accuracy is neural. ICLabel is also not trained on gait-related artefact specifically.

\textbf{Window length differs between cohorts.} Cohort B uses 2\,s windows
against cohort A's 4\,s, so the context pathway spans a shorter duration there.
This is a confound for the context ablation specifically, though not for the
rate condition, which is measured within each cohort.

\section{Conclusion}

An estimate that adapts at test time tracks whatever is currently streaming.
Under a protocol that presents each class in long contiguous blocks, the
estimate converges to the current class, and the layer then removes the signal
it was inserted to protect. The estimator's memory must therefore outlast a
class block. That bound is read from the recording protocol, which makes it a
design-time check.

The condition has predicted a new cohort correctly in advance. With class
blocks of five windows, enabling alignment cost nothing and slowing it brought
no gain, where slowing recovered $0.118$ over three seeds on the cohort with long
blocks. Where the lower bound holds, the gain from alignment is small and not significant on either cohort that satisfies it: $+0.026$ over three seeds on cohort A and $+0.014$ over two replication seeds on cohort C, and on cohort A the stem alone leads every arm on the mean over seeds 1 and 2, again without a significant difference. Where it fails, alignment costs $0.149$ over three seeds. The condition is therefore most useful as a design-time guard against that loss.

The immediate open question is the upper bound. Neither cohort here resolves
the drift timescale: cohort A's session covariances are already at their plateau
one session apart, and cohort B has too few trials to fit one. A protocol with
recordings spaced closely enough to sample the rise of drift, and with
$\tau_{\mathrm{blk}}$ between roughly 100 and 200 windows, would place the two
bounds close together, and the prediction is that accuracy would peak at an
intermediate momentum and fall on both sides. That experiment can be
pre-registered from the protocol alone, as the cohort C test was.

A second direction follows from the unexplained residual on cohort~B. If a single running estimate
cannot simultaneously avoid the class and track the drift, then the two
requirements should perhaps be served by two estimators at different rates, with
the fast one constrained to the subspace in which drift lives and the slow one
supplying the class-neutral reference. Whether such a decomposition is identifiable without labels remains open.

These observations may extend to other online adaptation settings. In five cases here a distribution-level quantity improved without a corresponding task improvement, and in three of those the assumption that it would had already been acted upon. Such measures are one-sided by construction, which suggests that methods justified by a reduced divergence should also report the accuracy cost of that reduction.
